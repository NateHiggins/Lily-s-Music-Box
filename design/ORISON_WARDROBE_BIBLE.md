# THE ORISON WARDROBE BIBLE

Appearance and fashion criteria for the eighteen residents, written so
that A-pose generation prompts can be derived directly from each entry.

Companion to `ORISON_BIBLE.md` §IV (identity, face, wound), §VIII (THE
DIVERGENCE) and `ORISON_RELATIONSHIP_WEB.md`. Where this document adds
backstory it *extends* those; it does not contradict them. Signature
garments named in the bible are canon and appear here unchanged.

**Re-dated to 1927, 2026-08-08.** The first version of this document was
written before the bible pinned the present to late 1927, and it dressed
eighteen people in nylon, lycra, poly-blend suiting, trainers, cargo
trousers, fleece and nitrile gloves — none of which existed. Every
garment below has been replaced with something a person could have
bought, inherited or mended in New York in 1927. The framework — three
states, one colour each, persistence over costume — was right and is
unchanged.

---

## I. THE RULE, APPLIED TO CLOTHES

The bible's engine is **both true**: the Orison is a real prewar block in
Queens *and* it is purgatory; the residents have leases *and* some have
been here for centuries. Neither reading is allowed to win.

Clothing obeys this or it breaks the game. Every outfit must read as
**ordinary contemporary dress** — a real person who bought real things
in a real borough, in 1927 — *and* as very slightly **out of time**, in a
way no single garment can be blamed for.

The way to achieve that is not costume. It is **persistence**: things
kept far past their replacement date, mended rather than replaced, worn
in a cut that stopped being current a while ago and was never updated.
A cardigan from a shop that closed. A coat re-lined twice. Shoes resoled
so often the shape has changed.

**In 1927 that has a specific and very useful meaning.** The war ended
nine years ago and the silhouette changed completely in between: a
woman still wearing 1913 — long skirt, high collar, uncut hair — is not
in fancy dress, she is a woman who stopped. A man in a sack suit cut in
1919 with a collar turned to hide the fray is every third man in Queens.
The out-of-time quality is free, because the period supplies it: the
gap between what a garment was cut for and the year it is being worn in
is *already* a decade wide for half this building.

Read one way, these are people who do not shop much. Read the other,
they are people for whom time has not been passing normally. **Both
true.**

Forbidden, because they resolve the ambiguity:
- **Costume-drama 1927.** No cigarette holders held aloft, no fringed
  dancing frocks, no gangster pinstripes. Nobody in this building is at
  a party. The decade's *iconography* is banned; its *tailoring* is
  mandatory.
- Loud current-season fashion of any era, visible brand marks, slogans.
- Anything supernatural, uncanny, or "wrong" as a garment. The clothes
  are never the horror. The clothes are the ordinary reading, held
  perfectly, for as long as possible.

## II. THE PERIOD, PRECISELY

Research basis for every entry below. Get the silhouette right and the
rest follows; get it wrong and no amount of wear detail rescues it.

### II.1 Women, 1927

**1927 is the extreme year.** The tubular *garçonne* line is at its
furthest: waist seam dropped to the **hip**, bust suppressed flat rather
than shaped, no defined waist anywhere. Hems are at their **shortest of
the entire decade — just below the knee**, about fifteen inches from the
floor. They begin dropping again in 1928 and are back at mid-calf by
1930. A woman in a 1927 hem is standing at the exact peak of something.

- **Day frock.** One piece, rayon crêpe or wool jersey, cut straight;
  side pleats, godets or a knife-pleated inset for walking room; a sash,
  buckle or tie at the hip. Collars and cuffs are often a detachable
  contrasting set, so they can be washed separately — a poor woman owns
  one frock and three collars.
- **The alternative silhouette:** the *robe de style*. Dropped waist,
  but a wide full skirt over hip supports. This is what a woman wears
  when the tube does not suit her — older, fuller, or simply refusing.
  Naming it is how you dress a woman over fifty without dating her to
  1913 by accident.
- **At home:** the house dress, printed cotton percale or gingham,
  button-through, ordered from a mail-order catalogue. Over it a bib
  apron or a wrap-around apron.
- **Underneath:** the *step-in* (chemise and knickers in one) in rayon or
  cotton; a **bandeau brassière that flattens**, not a shaping bra; a
  girdle or corselette of coutil with elastic panels; suspenders. A
  princess slip under a frock.
- **Stockings.** Silk if she can afford it, **rayon — "artificial silk" —
  if she cannot**, lisle for work and winter. Seamed, and the seam gets
  checked. Rolled below the knee on a garter if she is young and wants
  it noticed; gartered above if she does not.
- **Shoes.** Bar shoe (Mary Jane), T-strap, or Oxford for walking. Louis
  or Cuban heel, about two inches. Galoshes over them in winter.
- **The cloche**, pulled down to the eyebrows, and it is **not optional
  out of doors**. Felt in winter, straw in summer. It only works on
  short hair, which is why the hat and the bob arrived together — and
  why a woman who kept her hair long wears a **toque** instead, and
  everyone can read that off her at fifty feet.
- **Hair:** bob, then shingle (tapered into the nape), then the Eton
  crop. Marcel-waved with hot tongs or finger-waved wet.
- **Gloves out of doors, always.** One long rope of beads. A frame-clasp
  handbag.
- **Coat:** straight, buttoned or wrapped low on the hip, large shawl
  collar in fur or fur fabric.

### II.2 Men, 1927

- **The three-piece lounge suit.** Single-breasted, natural shoulder,
  high waist, trousers with turn-ups and a pressed crease, bottoms 19–21
  inches. **Oxford bags** at 22–25 inches are university fashion and read
  as young and faintly ridiculous.
- **Collars are the class tell.** Soft attached collars are winning, but
  older and more formal men still wear a **detachable stiff collar** —
  celluloid or starched linen — on a collarless shirt, fixed front and
  back with studs. **A collar turned inside out to hide the fray is the
  clearest poverty marker available**, and it is visible.
- **Waistcoat**, bottom button undone. A **pocket watch** on a chain
  across it if he is over forty; a wristwatch if he was in France and
  kept the habit.
- **Braces, not a belt.** Trousers cut for braces have no loops. A belt
  on an older man is simply wrong.
- **Hats:** fedora, homburg, derby for the formal and the ageing; **flat
  cap or newsboy for working men**; a straw boater in summer, already
  dating.
- **Shoes:** leather Oxfords and brogues; hobnailed or engineer boots for
  labour; spats surviving on old men.
- **Knitwear:** the Fair Isle pullover (Prince of Wales, 1922) and the
  sleeveless V-neck.
- **Work dress:** bib overalls in denim, chambray work shirt, moleskin
  trousers, a duck or canvas jacket, a **union suit** underneath, **arm
  garters** on a clerk to keep his cuffs out of the ink, **sock
  suspenders** on everyone.
- **Outerwear:** Chesterfield, ulster, raglan; a trench coat if he served.

### II.3 What does not exist yet

The most useful list in this document. Every one of these was in the
first draft.

| Not yet | Arrives |
|---|---|
| nylon | 1938 |
| polyester | 1941 |
| acrylic | 1950 |
| Lastex (elastic yarn) | 1931 |
| Lycra / spandex | 1958 |
| Velcro | 1955 |
| nitrile gloves | 1960s |
| zip fasteners **on clothing** | 1930s (the hookless fastener exists from 1913 — on galoshes, money belts and tobacco pouches, not on a frock or a fly) |
| the A-line | 1955, as a term and a cut |
| the stiletto heel | 1950s |
| trainers, tracksuits, hoodies, fleece, ripstop, cargo trousers, crossbody bags, baseball caps as civilian dress | all far later |

Also absent: **poly-cotton and any blend of that kind**; elastic
waistbands other than knitted or drawstring; the T-shirt as an outer
garment — in 1927 it is underwear and stays under; and blue jeans as
anything but workwear, where they are called overalls.

Available and worth using: wool serge, flannel, tweed, gabardine,
melton, jersey · cotton percale, gingham, voile, lawn, chambray, duck,
drill, moleskin, denim · linen · silk · **rayon, new, cheap, shiny and
everywhere** · acetate · lisle · oilcloth · rubberised mackintosh cloth
· celluloid and Bakelite for buttons, buckles and collars.

### II.4 One licensed exception

Bible §VIII.2, the Rule of Signal: *does it carry, capture, switch, store
or reproduce a signal?* If yes, it is forty years ahead of its time.

That applies to worn objects too, and it is the only place this document
is allowed to break period. **Cal's hearing aid and Juno's recorder are
signal devices and may look 1967.** Everything else either side of them
is 1927 and probably second-hand.

## III. THE THREE STATES

Each resident gets three models. They are not three outfits — they are
three degrees of **presentation**, and the character is what survives
between them.

**1. STREET — going out to the shop and back.**
Fully presented: outerwear, hat, gloves, hair done, whatever face they
put on. Not "dressed up" — this is the twenty-minute errand, which is
exactly why it is revealing. What does this person consider the
*minimum* acceptable state for being seen by neighbours? In 1927 that
minimum includes a hat and gloves for almost everyone, so **whoever goes
out without them is making a statement** and it should be deliberate.
Carries the thing they never leave without.

**2. LATE NIGHT — dishevelled, in their own apartment, 3 a.m.**
The hour the game lives in. Nobody is coming over. Layers shed,
softened, mismatched; the day's structure abandoned but not replaced.
Feet are the tell — bare, stockinged, carpet slippers trodden flat.
Hair released from whatever held it. Collars and cuffs unpinned and set
aside. This is the state most residents are actually *in* when the
player meets them, so it carries the most weight.

**3. TOWEL — interrupted in the shower.**
A single towel, wet hair, no powder, no jewellery except what never
comes off. The purpose is not exposure; it is the removal of every
authored signal at once. Whatever still reads as this person here —
posture, hair, the mark on the skin, the one ring they never take off —
is the irreducible character. Model modestly and practically: towel
secured, natural stance, the expression of someone who has answered a
door they wish they hadn't.

## IV. WHAT EVERY A-POSE PROMPT MUST CARRY

These are mesh-and-texture source images. The pipeline is the same as
the existing dump route (`art/blender/meshy/<set>/` →
`convert_dump_characters.py` → retarget onto the shared 22-joint
resident skeleton via `resident_moves.glb`), so the images must be
generation-friendly first and beautiful second.

Every prompt states, in this order:

1. **A-pose, standing, symmetrical**, arms down and out at roughly 45°,
   palms facing the body, feet shoulder-width, weight even, looking
   straight ahead. No contrapposto, no attitude, no crossed limbs.
2. **Full body, head to below the feet, nothing cropped**, orthographic
   or long-lens front view, subject centred, plain flat mid-grey
   background.
3. **Flat even diffuse studio lighting, no cast shadows, no rim light,
   no dramatic key.** Shadow baked into a texture is a defect forever.
4. **Neutral face, mouth closed, eyes open, no expression.**
5. The garments, top to bottom, **each with fabric, cut, colour and
   wear state** — never "a jacket", always "a boiled-wool car coat,
   bottle green, cuffs gone shiny, one button replaced in the wrong
   shade".
6. Hair and face state.
7. Footwear (or bare feet), stated explicitly — feet are cropped or
   invented otherwise.
8. **No props held in the hands.** The A-pose must keep hands clear.
   Signature items are worn, pocketed, or slung — never gripped.
9. **Say the year.** "1927" in the prompt does more work than any other
   single word, and without it the generator defaults to now.
10. Closing tech: *neutral colours under neutral light, no stylisation,
    no filter, photoreal, consistent character, full figure visible.*

## V. SHARED VOCABULARY

Use these exact terms so eighteen characters stay in one world.

**Wear states**, weakest to strongest: *fresh · settled (washed soft,
holding shape) · shiny (cuffs, elbows, seat) · thin (weave visible at
stress points) · mended (a repair that matches) · visibly repaired (a
repair that does not match, and was allowed to show) · failing.*

**Period-specific wear worth naming:** a **turned collar** (unpicked and
reversed to hide the fray) · a **re-cut** garment (a man's suit taken in
for a smaller man, the old seam line still visible) · **re-lined** (a
lining that does not match the outside, and shows when the coat swings)
· **resoled** so often the shape has changed · **darned** at the heel of
a stocking, where it does not show inside a shoe.

**Palette discipline.** The building is plaster cream, oak amber, dull
brass, cold night blue. Residents are muted and desaturated against it,
each owning **one** colour that is theirs. No two adjacent units share a
signature colour. Nobody wears pure black or pure white — both read as
graphic-design, not laundry.

**Fabrics that carry the world:** boiled wool, wool serge, flannel,
tweed, melton, jersey, corduroy, moleskin, cotton duck, drill, chambray,
percale, gingham, voile, lawn, lisle, rayon crêpe, oilcloth, terry.
**Fabrics to avoid:** everything in §II.3.

### 1A — Evelyn Marsh · retired teacher
**Extension.** Taught fourth grade thirty-one years in the same
building; retired the year they repainted her room and did not tell her.
Buys clothes to last and is quietly proud of how long she has made them
last. Irons things nobody will see.
**Colour** plum. **Silhouette** neat, closed, buttoned to the second
button — and **pre-war**: she never shortened her hems and never cut her
hair, and both are visible from across a street. **Fabrics** lambswool,
cotton voile, tweed. **Hair/face** grey, long, pinned up in a low coil;
a little powder, no more. **Signature** red pencil.

- **STREET** — Plum lambswool cardigan, settled, over an ironed cream
  cotton voile blouse with a high collar; grey wool skirt **to mid-calf**,
  the 1913 length she never gave up; fawn gabardine coat, unbelted;
  black lisle stockings; flat brown lace-up Oxfords resoled twice; a
  small stiff frame-clasp handbag; grey cotton gloves. **A felt toque,
  not a cloche** — a cloche needs cut hair. Red pencil through the coil
  above the ear.
- **LATE NIGHT** — Same cardigan over a long flannel nightgown, sleeves
  pushed up; hand-knitted bed socks; carpet slippers trodden flat. Hair
  in a single grey plait. Spectacles pushed up. Pencil on her, still.
- **TOWEL** — Cream huckaback towel, tucked and firmly held. Wet grey
  hair **down past her shoulders**, and the length is the shock — nobody
  has ever seen it. No spectacles. Thin gold wedding band she has not
  removed since 1901.

### 1D — Teresa Vale · night nurse
**Extension.** Twenty-two years of nights. Sleeps 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. and
does the shop on the way home in yesterday's uniform. Owns nothing that
needs ironing that she has not already ironed.
**Colour** navy. **Silhouette** utilitarian, pinned, nothing loose.
**Fabrics** cotton uniform cloth, wool, starched linen. **Hair/face**
dark hair scraped into a low bun, flyaway; bare face, permanent
under-eye shadow. **Signature** a dented vacuum flask; the fob watch.

- **STREET** — **Navy wool nurse's cape with the scarlet lining**, over
  yesterday's grey-blue cotton uniform dress; the starched apron off and
  folded into the bag; detachable white collar and cuffs still pinned on
  because unpinning them is a decision; black lisle stockings; black
  laced shoes, low heel, polished at the toe only. Cap crushed into a
  pocket. Flask under one arm.
- **LATE NIGHT** — Uniform dress with the collar and cuffs **unpinned and
  visible in frame**; a man's cardigan over it; stockinged feet, no
  shoes. Bun collapsed. Blackout blind down at four in the afternoon.
- **TOWEL** — Navy towel. Wet hair flat. Bare face. **The fob watch is
  pinned to the towel's edge** — she moved it across without thinking,
  because she times things without deciding to.

### 2A — Mina Vale · caption editor, former court reporter
**Extension.** Twenty-two years certified; her ears began editing the
record and four blank seconds ended it. Dresses as though still due in
court — sharp, contained, correct — for work she does alone in a room.
**Colour** slate. **Silhouette** the **tailor-made**: jacket and skirt,
structured, everything aligned. **Fabrics** worsted wool, cotton
shirting. **Hair/face** dark shingle, blunt and exactly level; powder,
defined brow. **Signature** a stenographer's pencil behind the ear.

- **STREET** — Slate worsted tailored jacket, hip-length, shoulders
  holding their shape, one button; white cotton shirtwaist, soft collar
  crisp; straight slate skirt **to just below the knee, exact**; grey
  cloche worn level; grey kid gloves; low Cuban-heel bar shoes polished.
  Small frame handbag. Overdressed for the shop and aware of it.
- **LATE NIGHT** — Shirtwaist untucked and half-unbuttoned over a rayon
  step-in; cardigan slipping off one shoulder; bare feet. Hair behind
  one ear. Face half-removed — one eye done, one clean. The most undone
  anyone in this building gets.
- **TOWEL** — Grey towel. Wet hair pushed back, the blunt shingle
  obvious. Bare face, and the contrast is severe: without the line she
  looks years younger and much less certain.

### 2B — Lena Ortiz · seamstress
**Extension.** Alters for half the block and refuses payment from three
of them. Her own hems are pinned, not sewn — she means to get to them.
**Colour** burgundy. **Silhouette** layered, soft, sleeves always
pushed. **Fabrics** knit, cotton voile, wool suiting offcuts.
**Hair/face** black hair in a low twist held by a scrap of selvedge;
bare, warm. **Signature** shears; a pincushion band at the wrist.

- **STREET** — Burgundy knitted cardigan-coat, **visibly repaired at both
  elbows in contrasting wool and proud of it**; a printed cotton voile
  frock she cut herself, dropped waist, **one hem still pinned**; rayon
  stockings, one darned at the heel; low bar shoes. Tape measure round

- **LATE NIGHT** — Same cardigan over a rayon princess slip, belted with

- **TOWEL** — Deep red towel. Wet black hair down, **much longer than
  anyone sees** — she never cut it and covers it daily. The pincushion
  band is *off*; this is the only state where it is, and that is the
  point.

**Extension.** Perpetually about to leave. Never fully unpacked, so they
wear the top layer of the case.
**Colour** none of their own — greys and whatever was on top.
**Silhouette** mismatched, travel-creased. **Fabrics** cheap ready-made
worsted, cotton. **Hair/face** indeterminate, tired.
**Signature** mismatched luggage; **a railway cloakroom ticket still
knotted to a buttonhole.**

- **STREET** — Both in creased layers that agree with neither each other
  nor the season; one in a cheap ready-made suit **still showing the
  fold-marks of the shop's paper**; the other in an overcoat with the
  cloakroom ticket on the buttonhole. A cardboard case with a strap and
  a gladstone bag that do not match.
- **LATE NIGHT** — One in a borrowed nightshirt, one still in
  yesterday's shirt. Case open on the floor, still packed.
- **TOWEL** — Mismatched towels, one clearly the building's. Wet hair.
  Nothing personal on either of them at all — no ring, no chain, no
  mark. *They carry no signature, and that is the character.*

### 5A — Nadia Quell · architect
**Extension.** Was silenced about violations once and signs the welcome
letter "Management" rather than her name. Dresses for authority she is
not sure she still has — and in 1927 a woman architect is armouring
herself against a room that will assume she is the typist.
**Colour** oxblood. **Silhouette** sharp, tailored, one bold line.
**Fabrics** heavy wool, structured cotton. **Hair/face** dark shingle
pulled back severely; strong brow, oxblood lip. **Signature** a boxwood
folding rule in the coat pocket.

- **STREET** — Oxblood wool coat, sharply cut, buttoned low on the hip;
  black high-necked jersey frock beneath; **black cloche worn level**;
  oxblood kid gloves; plain low-heeled bar shoes. Folding rule in the
  pocket, protruding. Reads as somebody going to a meeting, buying milk.
- **LATE NIGHT** — The frock with the coat gone and the pressed line
  collapsed; hair released; lip gone. Barefoot on a floor she probably
  has opinions about.
- **TOWEL** — Oxblood towel. Wet hair back. Bare face, and the brow is
  still the strongest thing about her.

### 5B — Cal Dwyer · radio collector
**Extension.** Tunes to preserve moments by preventing their ending. The
cardigan is the one he was wearing on a night he will not describe.
**Colour** mustard. **Silhouette** soft, sagged, pocket-heavy.
**Fabrics** thick hand-knitted wool, brushed flannel. **Hair/face**
white hair, soft and uncombed. **Signature** the hearing aid — **in all
three states**, because it is not a costume choice.

> **The aid is a §II.4 device and should look it.** Not a beige modern
> shell: a **black Bakelite receiver at the ear on a brown cloth cord**
> running down into the cardigan pocket, where the battery case sits.
> That is what sags the pocket, and it is why the cardigan hangs the way
> it does — the garment's whole silhouette is explained by the object.

- **STREET** — Mustard hand-knitted cardigan, one pocket sagging with
  the battery case and the other with spare valves, elbows gone thin;
  checked flannel shirt buttoned to the throat with **no collar attached
  at all**; brown trousers on braces; slip-on shoes gone down at the
  heel; flat cap. Receiver clear of the ear, cord visible.
- **LATE NIGHT** — Same cardigan over a nightshirt — he does not take it
  off; carpet slippers flattened past use. Hair up on one side. **Aid
  still in, at 3 a.m.**, which says everything.
- **TOWEL** — Mustard towel gone pale. Wet white hair. **Aid out** —
  receiver, cord and battery case coiled on the shelf beside him, in
  frame. The one state where he cannot hear, and the only image of him
  without it.

### 5C — Iris Bell · painter
**Extension.** Works for imagined audiences and cannot stop performing
for them. Her overalls are the most honest object she owns.
**Colour** ultramarine. **Silhouette** loose, wrapped, sleeves shoved.
**Fabrics** cotton duck, jersey, printed cotton. **Hair/face** hair
wrapped in a printed scarf; paint on the hands and one cheekbone.
**Signature** the ruined overalls; the scarf.

- **STREET** — Paint-ruined cotton-duck **painter's overalls**, decades
  of layered colour, worn *out of doors* — she has stopped noticing; a
  long knitted cardigan over; printed cotton headscarf tied at the nape;
  canvas sand-shoes stiff with dried paint. Ultramarine dominates the
  accretion.
- **LATE NIGHT** — Overalls off the shoulders and tied at the waist by
  the sleeves; a cotton chemise; bare feet with paint on them. Scarf
  off, hair a flattened mass.
- **TOWEL** — Blue towel. Wet hair, far longer than the scarf implies.
  Paint **still** on the hands and under the nails after washing — the
  one thing that does not come off.

### 6A — Sacha Reed · photographer-documentarian (they/them)
**Extension.** The recording displaced the experience; there is almost
no photograph of them. Dresses in what has pockets and does not rustle
— which in 1927 means men's working clothes, worn without explanation.
**Colour** slate green. **Silhouette** utilitarian, strapped, layered.
**Fabrics** cotton duck, drill, leather. **Hair/face** cropped close at
the back, longer on top; bare face, sharp eyes.
**Signature** a tangle of leather straps and brass fittings at the neck.

- **STREET** — Slate-green cotton-duck coat with too many pockets, all
  used; collarless shirt; dark cotton drill trousers; low canvas boots;
  a soft cloth cap. Two leather straps crossed at the chest with cable
  releases, filter cases and a lens hood clipped to them. **No camera
  body visible** — the tangle without the camera is the character.
- **LATE NIGHT** — Shirt and loose knitted shorts, one strap *still*
  round the neck out of habit; socks. Hair up on one side.
- **TOWEL** — Slate towel. Wet hair pushed back. The strap is off, and
  the mark where it crosses the neck is the only thing on them.

### 6B — Jonah Price · insomniac writer
**Extension.** Avoids endings until they bite. Has not fully dressed on
a weekday in a long time; the dressing gown has become the outfit.
**Colour** navy. **Silhouette** enveloped, corded, collar up.
**Fabrics** heavy wool, brushed flannel. **Hair/face** unbrushed brown
hair, a week unshaven; ink on the fingers.
**Signature** the navy dressing gown; an annotated notebook in the
pocket.

- **STREET** — Navy dressing gown replaced — barely — by a navy overcoat
  worn **over pyjama trousers**, which is the joke and the tragedy;
  flannel shirt, no collar attached; unlaced shoes; **no hat**, which in
  1927 is close to going out undressed and everyone on the stair can
  tell. Notebook in the coat pocket, corner out.
- **LATE NIGHT** — The dressing gown, corded twice, over a nightshirt;
  bare shins; carpet slippers trodden flat. This is his default and it
  looks it.
- **TOWEL** — Navy towel. Wet hair pushed straight back off the face,
  which makes him look startlingly awake. Ink still on two fingers.

### 6C — Mae Kessler · antiques appraiser
**Extension.** Certainty is not memory: she has authenticated things she
now doubts. Dresses in objects she has appraised and quietly kept.
**Colour** bottle green. **Silhouette** long, structured, buttoned —
Edwardian bones under a 1927 coat. **Fabrics** wool melton, silk lining,
kid leather. **Hair/face** silver hair set and pinned; powder, a soft
dark lip. **Signature** white cotton gloves — hers are **manners**, not
work; see Noel (4C).

- **STREET** — Bottle-green wool melton coat to mid-calf, **re-lined
  twice** (a flash of a lining that does not match the outside);
  high-collared blouse with a mourning brooch at the throat; long grey
  skirt she has never shortened; **polished buttoned boots**, with a
  buttonhook living in the handbag; a small toque, not a cloche. White
  cotton gloves, worn.
- **LATE NIGHT** — Coat gone, blouse unbuttoned at the throat, brooch
  off and *placed* in frame; long cardigan; stockinged feet. Hair
  released from its pins, still holding the shape of the set.
- **TOWEL** — Green towel. Wet silver hair, the set gone completely.
  Gloves off, and the hands are much older than the face she maintains.
  This is the most exposed image in the building.

## VII. SEQUENCING

54 models is too many to commit to blind. Take **one character fully
through the pipeline first** — image → mesh → texture → retarget onto
the shared skeleton → in-engine at the right body scale → walk them —
and only then batch the rest.

Recommended first subject: **Cal Dwyer (5B)**. He carries a persistent
signature across all three states, one of which removes it, so he tests
the hardest continuity rule; mustard against navy night lighting is a
real palette test; and his aid is the one §II.4 device in the building,
so getting it wrong is cheap to discover now and expensive later.

Then batch by floor, in this order — F01, F02, F03 (the units the player
reaches first), then F04–F06.

**Verify before batching:**
- that the retarget holds on a figure in a **long coat** (Mae) and in a
  **dressing gown** (Jonah). Loose hanging garments are where the shared
  skeleton is most likely to break, and finding that out on model 54 is
  the expensive way;
- that **a cloche reads as a cloche** at gameplay distance and not as a
  helmet. Six of the nine women wear one, so if it fails it fails six
  times;
- that **a hem just below the knee** survives the retarget without
  riding up into a 1960s skirt, which is the single fastest way to lose
  the period.
