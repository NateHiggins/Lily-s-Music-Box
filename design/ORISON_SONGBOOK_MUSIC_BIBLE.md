## 1. THE THESIS

**The song is old; the backing is honest; the voice comes back a century
later, too fast — and it rips.**

Every song in this collection is a real composition a person standing in
Queens in 1928 could know by heart, performed as an honest period
recording: a named band, a named room, wire hiss and all, at its own
speed and pitch. That is what the player hears at the terminal and sings
over on the stage.

The nightcore is what the future does to them. When a take is published,
the Songbook's far end reconstructs the whole thing — singer, backing,
room — **deliberately too fast, tempo and pitch rising together**, no
formant correction, one tuned ratio stored forever with that version.
The band turns frantic; the singer comes back bright, funny and
unmistakably chipmunked; a sincere performance is returned in the wrong
body and it absolutely moves. This is the First Sounds mistake — the
2008 too-fast reading that heard Scott's own slow song as a woman's —
canonized as the network's house dialect (§III.2, with citations).
Nightcore as a playback accident, not as a genre anyone in 1928 chose.

The register to hold for every returned version: **sincere 2007
internet nightcore energy.** Euphoric, embarrassing, romantic,
melodramatic, completely unashamed — the compilation-upload spirit in
which old "cringe" nightcore is celebrated as pure dance music. If a
returned version sounds respectful before it sounds sick, the ratio is
wrong or the song is.

Every track answers one question before any other:

> **When this comes back too fast — would somebody have uploaded that
> in 2007 with an anime still, and would we play it twice?**

If not, it is cut. The base recording must also stand on its own at
native speed (people sing over it all night), but the return is the
test, because the return is what the community actually shares.

## 2. THE MACHINES, AND THE TWO LAYERS

One canon rule governs everything here (§III.2, binding): **the
instrumental catalogue and the published version are different audio
layers and must never be collapsed.**

| Machine | Job |
|---|---|
| **The television** (F15: the last TV in the game) | Lyric and phrase guide — slot shapes, syllable maps, prompts; under the phrase-duel brief, the lyric book assembled from learned phrase cards. Presentation only; no canonical lyrics exist |
| **The PA** (`BarPA` bus) | Plays base instrumentals at authored speed for writing and performing; plays published community versions — which arrive already reconstructed, chipmunk and all — when the room calls one up |
| **The phonautograph** (§III.2) | Records the live take as a physical trace. The 1857 mechanism itself never plays audio — terminals and readers emit reconstructions; the horn and cylinder do not |
| **The Songbook** (binder + terminal) | The ledger: versions, ancestry, lyric books, and each published version's immutable `reconstruction_ratio` |
| **The basement studio** (S-section, proposal; G2 as corrected) | Restoration and found-trace work — the scholarly end of the pipeline, no longer the sole playback site; community versions are receivable wherever the Songbook is |

Boundary consequences:

- A chipmunk voice **in a playing community version** is catalog
  behavior. Uncommanded singing when no version is playing is the
  Tenant (§III.2, binding).
- The too-fast transform applies to the **complete version** — voice
  and backing together. A chipmunk vocal over an untouched backing is
  the wrong effect and is named as such in canon.
- The untransformed mic stem may exist locally for assembly and
  latency; nothing ever shares a surprise clean recording of the
  player.
- **No base instrumental is pre-chipmunked.** The catalogue spans
  styles, tempi and registers at honest speed; the nightcore belongs
  to returns. (A base may be *fast* — the Charleston is the
  Charleston — but native fast is not the artifact.)
- Found historical traces may carry separately authored, unstable
  reconstruction behavior (the same trace reading as two people on
  two nights). That instability is found-object color only; it never
  applies to published player versions, whose ratio never rerolls.

## 3. THE OBJECTS — one pipeline, five things

1. **THE SOURCE** — the historical composition: real title, real
   writer, real year, documented public-domain score.
2. **THE BASE** — the catalogue instrumental: in-fiction a period
   recording (a named ensemble, a named room, cut to wire, reaching
   the bar by ordinary hands), authored by us as real audio, played
   at its own speed. Karaoke skeleton per the songbook brief: lead
   absent or ghosted, guide quiet, sections and phrase slots defined.
3. **THE TAKE** — the live performance: singer + base + room, one
   complete event, physically a trace on smoked paper.
4. **THE PUBLISHED VERSION** — the take sent through the century
   jump: one tuned `reconstruction_ratio > 1.0`, tempo and pitch
   together, stored immutably. Everyone who receives it hears the
   same gloriously wrong artifact.
5. **THE FOUND TRACE** — pre-existing soot with authored rules of its
   own (G4): unstable readers allowed, ambiguity allowed, ownership
   unknown. Found objects, not pipeline.

Provenance texture (kept short by owner order): spools wear paper obi
labels, wrong catalog numbers, hand-wound tails and other people's
handwriting. Nobody who cut these records is a touring star — the
cross section in §11 runs to a night operator, a piano tuner, stranded
vaudevillians, a dance-hall floor manager, two laundresses, a
settlement glee club, a soda-fountain clerk, a notary's clerk,
produce-cart brothers, a Pullman porter, messenger kids and a seltzer
route. The impossible thing is never the paperwork; it is only what
the far end of the Songbook does to a take.

## 4. THE CURATION RUBRIC

A song enters Volume One only if it clears all six:

1. **THE 2007 TEST, applied to the return.** At its house ratio the
   returned version must rip — anime-still-upload energy, played
   twice. Unappealable.
2. **Period-available.** Published or commonly sung by 1928; the
   base recording is period-honest. The *return* is the
   impossibility; the songs and the catalogue never are.
3. **Documented and free.** Composition ≤1930 US publication (public
   domain as of 2026), named writers, archival score source;
   author-death terms checked and flagged GREEN/AMBER per §13.
   Uncertain → BLOCKED, not rationalized.
4. **Singable by a tired stranger at native speed.** Strong phrases,
   generous slots, a chorus that survives bad singing — and whose
   hook gets bigger, not thinner, when the return speeds it.
5. **Socially inhabited.** At least two named residents choose it
   for reasons that are theirs.
6. **Load-bearing in the spread.** Across twelve: joy, bad taste,
   gender euphoria, ordinary romance, night-shift solidarity, family
   inheritance, labor, friendship, embarrassment, dancing,
   heartbreak. No register over a third; marginalized lives in the
   lineage appear at every register, not only the sad ones.

## 5. THE SOUND

### 5.1 The base catalogue (native speed, varied on purpose)

Twelve honest period recordings, deliberately spread: parlor waltz,
after-hours trio, hapa haole quartet, hot dance band with caller,
laundry-loft duet, settlement-house crowd, pharmacy-counter quintet,
notary-office deadpan, produce-cart novelty, stairwell torch band,
bridge-walk string band, seltzer-depot family. House rules:

- **Period instrumentation only**, honestly played: no drum machines,
  no synthesis, no modern kit sound. Wire-era grit, room bleed and
  incident (laughs, bells, one el train) are kept — the catalogue's
  texture is documentary, not lo-fi cosplay.
- **Affinity layers are period-plausible and light.** A performed
  stairwell break, a bass-drum four-on-the-floor overdub, a spliced
  double chorus, a doubled rest — one habit per track at most, all
  physically possible in 1928, chosen because the *return* will
  reveal them as jungle chop, hardcore kick, hyperpop gag. Some
  tracks take none: an excellent song, played straight, is a
  complete design (owner rule).
- **Karaoke skeleton behavior** per the brief: lead ghosted or
  absent, one quiet guide instrument, four-to-eight-bar phrase
  slots, section metadata. The skeleton must groove at native speed
  — people spend whole evenings here before anything is published.

### 5.2 The return (the published version's one transform)

The whole take — voice, base, room — through real varispeed, pitch
and tempo locked, once, forever. House ratio guidance:

| Ratio | Pitch rise | Returned feel |
|---|---|---|
| ×1.19 | +3 semitones | shy; only for bases already hot |
| ×1.26 | +4 | the floor of the house sound |
| ×1.335 | +5 | the center — bright, breathless, correct |
| ×1.414 | +6 | the ceiling; voices go full glitter |

- **No formant correction. Ever.** The chipmunk is the dialect.
- **No time-stretch, no independent pitch, no per-listen reroll** —
  all named in canon; the ratio is part of the version's identity.
- The proposal's per-track "house ratio" is a tuned default for
  system-seeded versions and a recommendation the runtime may apply
  to player publications; who finally owns tuning is §16-Q3.
- Artifacts to embrace at the return: hiss lifted into a bright
  ribbon, compression pumping against rhythms that grew a kick,
  splices landing mid-word, incident sounds turning comic (a door
  latch becomes a switch; a metronome becomes a woodblock sprint).
- Artifacts to refuse: anything added *after* the speedup to
  modernize it — pads, sub-bass, sidechain gloss, de-essing. The
  return is a misreading, not a remix.

## 6. REAL-WORLD ATTRIBUTION — the ethics rules

The research behind this collection is of real artists and scenes —
disproportionately trans, nonbinary, queer, Black, Brown, immigrant
and disabled makers, because that is who actually built these
grammars and communities. The rules for carrying that respectfully:

1. **Nondiegetic attribution ships.** The Listening Line (§14) names
   the real artists and scenes in the game's extras with links to
   official listening/purchase pages. Fictionalizing influences
   without naming them brings light to no one.
2. **No imitation.** No brief names a living artist or describes one
   narrowly enough to commission a copy. Briefs are technique
   language only.
3. **No fictional double of a real person.** Fictional musicians get
   their own biographies, day jobs and flaws, audited in §15 against
   the lineage for accidental portraiture.
4. **Identity language only from self-description or strong public
   documentation**, cited in the research doc. Thin record → omit,
   not guess.
5. **No demographic decoration.** An artist appears because a
   concrete technique, practice or community insight was learned
   from them.
6. **No sampling, no famous breaks.** No commercial recording is
   sampled; no famous break is used or recreated-by-ear; breaks are
   newly performed. The documented history of uncompensated break
   musicians is the reason (research doc §V).
7. **No tragedy quota.** Artists are cited for what they built.
   Deaths are not the story; disability is practice, not
   inspiration; nobody is an identity label plus a genre.
8. **Compositions, not recordings.** Sources come from public-domain
   scores; historical recordings are never used, even ancient ones.
   Base-master vocals (where a ghost lead exists) sing only
   US-public-domain text; the in-game phrase guide is newly written
   (no canonical lyrics); dossiers quote at most a title line.
9. **2007 sincerity, not 2007 mockery.** The upload aesthetic is
   celebrated the way its own communities celebrate it — as pure
   dance music made with total conviction — never as a bit.

## 7. RHEA — supported, not centered

Rhea Sato (3D, vocal coach; wound: *mistakes accumulate as a captive
note*; rule: *imperfection can be voluntary*) is the resident this
collection touches most and must not be about. Rules:

- The collection gives her case instruments, not chapters: a penciled
  phrase guide, a song she refuses, a student's mislabeled trace —
  one line of pressure per dossier at most, never a quest.
- The pipeline is her perfect enemy at both ends: the trace keeps
  every take and deletes nothing, and **publication guarantees the
  returned version is not the voice she rehearsed** — every cover
  comes back chipmunked, hers included. A room where nobody's take
  survives with its dignity intact is the one room she can stand.
  That observation belongs to her dialogue someday, not to the UI.
- Her case machinery (`rhea_bad_karaoke`, `humiliation_named`,
  `imperfection_voluntary`) may later use traces and returned
  versions as evidence; nothing here pre-builds that.
- Distribution rule: at most four of twelve dossiers carry a Rhea
  line, and hers is never a track's emotional headline.

## 8. THE WRONG BODY — speed, gender, and the return

Canon supplies the sentence this section is built on: *a sincere
performance sent across a century and returned in the wrong body*
(§III.2). Rules:

1. **Nothing genders a voice as fact.** No UI, label or NPC line
   states the sex or age of a returned or reconstructed voice as
   fact. The historical precedent is canonized in-engine: the world
   heard Scott's own slow song as a woman's for a year.
2. **The return is stable wrongness.** A published version's ratio
   never rerolls — the wrong body is *the same* wrong body every
   time, which means it can be lived in, learned, even preferred.
   The collection's canonical euphoria beat (dossier 1): a resident
   who has never once replayed their own voice on purpose replays
   their returned version nightly — the far end's mistake is the
   first rendering of themselves they recognize.
3. **Found traces keep the roulette.** Only found historical traces
   read differently night to night (G4, authored); for them the
   uncertainty is grief and mercy by turns, and the studio will not
   adjudicate. Player versions are never roulette.
4. **The lineage is honest about why this lands.** Pitch-shifted
   listening as gendered self-recognition is documented practice in
   the real nightcore and hyperpop communities this collection
   studies (research doc §§II, IV); the Listening Line says the
   connection out loud.
5. **Rhea's case may use the wrongness; nothing may resolve it.**

## 9. SOCIAL USE — how the bar holds the songs

- Each dossier names which residents choose the track and why, plus
  one mundane anecdote in the house register (dry, tender, precise —
  the WORS catalog voice). The anecdotes are the deliverable; the
  tracks are how they happened.
- **Returned versions are the social currency.** The room's habit is
  to perform at native speed and then gather to hear what the far
  end did to it — the reveal of a fresh return is a nightly
  ceremony, equal parts christening and roast. Regulars have named
  relationships to their own returns: proud, feuding, resigned,
  secretly devoted.
- Etiquette is unwritten and absolute: nobody sings dossier 2 before
  midnight; dossier 9 is legally mandatory at birthdays; the barman
  has heard dossier 5 more times than any human should and hums it
  anyway.
- Traces are social objects — gifting one is intimacy, reading one
  without its singer present is a named faux pas, and the oldest
  spools and traces have no author and everyone has a theory.
- **Lyric books come from listening** (phrase-duel brief): phrase
  cards learned from residents' own speech fill the slots, so a
  song's words are the building talking to itself. The TV guide
  presents the assembled book; this proposal's slot shapes and
  prompts are written to host that system without depending on it.
- The PA belongs to the room: no DJ, the queue is the binder's
  ribbon, and two people fighting over the ribbon is drama the
  design endorses.

### TRACK_101 — DREAMLAND (DON'T WAIT UP)

- **Source:** "Meet Me Tonight in Dreamland" — music Leo Friedman,
  lyrics Beth Slater Whitson, pub. Chicago 1909 (Will Rossiter). Score:
  Levy Sheet Music Collection; Library of Congress; Internet Archive
  scan. **PD: GREEN** (US 1909; Friedman d. 1927, Whitson d. 1930 —
  life+70 clear everywhere).
- **Why it belongs:** the great ordinary-romance waltz — two million
  sheets sold, the tune every resident's parents courted to. At native
  speed it is a slow spin with your hand on somebody's shoulder;
  returned, *meet me tonight* stops being an invitation and becomes a
  countdown. Both lives of the song are worth having, which is the
  whole two-layer design in one track.

**A. BASE — 3/4, F major, 96 BPM, 2:41.** In-fiction: "The Dreamland
Waltz," cut 1926 in the back room of a Corona wedding-photography
studio by ten wedding-gig regulars — two violins, cornet, trombone,
clarinet, tuba, banjo, brushed traps, upright piano — with a tenor
ghost-lead, high, plain, unornamented. Small hard room, cornet-heavy
balance because the horn player owned the studio. Skeleton: tenor at
−14 dB as guide; cornet answers promoted between phrases; AABA, 16-bar
verse, two chorus passes, four chorus slots (8-8-8-10, rise-rise-fall-
arc). Kept incident: one chair scrape at bar 40; the bench is loose.

**B. THE RETURN — ×1.335, +5 → A♭, 128 in a one-beat whirl.** The
tenor ghost comes back birdlike; the brushes turn to rain; the tuba's
pulse gets quick enough that the compressor audibly pumps against it.
The singer — whoever they were — arrives bright, urgent and younger
than they stood. House habit: fresh returns of Dreamland are played at
the end of the reveal ceremony, because nothing follows it well.

- **The television shows:** the four chorus slots with prompt "WHERE
  SHOULD THEY MEET YOU?"; phrase-duel lyric books slot naturally here
  (meeting-places are exactly what residents' speech is full of).
- **The trace records:** singer loud, band faint and bright, glasses,
  the chair scrape — five different traces all carry it.
- **Generation brief (base only; no artist names):** record a complete
  3/4 dance-orchestra performance, 96 BPM, F major, ten pieces as
  specced, tenor ghost-lead singing the 1909 public-domain text at
  −14 dB, one small hard room, light era-honest surface noise, no
  edits, no fade. **Negative constraints:** no modern drums, no
  synthesis, no reverb beyond the room, no vocal tuning, no click.
- **Fictional provenance:** recorded by Adeline Percy, 24, night
  operator at the Newtown exchange, who moonlights recording weddings
  on the exchange's test recorder and cut the band she hears every
  Saturday anyway. Spool: NIGHT SHIFT No. 1, obi label in exchange-form
  typeface. Reached the Harukiya as a cornetist's tab collateral; the
  tab was paid; the barman kept the spool.
- **Residents who choose it:** Cam Ortiz (winds down to it after late
  runs, still slightly too fast for a waltz); Teresa Vale (requests
  it, does not sing it — night-shift solidarity with a recordist she
  has never met). The player's own desk shift adopts it by osmosis.
- **Mundane anecdote:** the Transient Guests performed it as a duet
  and left before publishing; the unclaimed take sits in the binder
  as "for whoever." Nobody publishes someone else's take. It waits.
- **Euphoria beat (the collection's canonical one, §8):** a resident
  who has never replayed their own voice on purpose publishes a
  Dreamland take, hears the return — bright, quick, wrong — and
  recognizes it at once. They play it nightly. Sacha (they/them, per
  the cast table) may carry this if the owner wants it named; the
  design works unnamed.
- **Researched lineage:** the founding nightcore practice of whole-
  recording varispeed (Nightcore of Alta, Norway, 2001-02); the
  documented trans listening culture around pitch-shifted voices; the
  First Sounds too-fast reading canonized in §III.2. Citations:
  research doc §§II, VI. Adeline Percy is a fictional 1920s operator
  and resembles no real person.

### TRACK_102 — THE GONDOLA SONG (LIFE IS BRIEF)

- **Source:** "Gondola no Uta" — music Nakayama Shinpei, lyrics Yoshii
  Isamu, 1915, Taishō Japan; the "inochi mijikashi" ballad. Score:
  period Japanese sheet editions; Nakayama scholarship (research doc
  §I). **PD: GREEN for the music** (US 1915; Japan life+50 expired
  2003, unrevived). Lyrics (Yoshii d. 1960) PD in US/Japan, life+70
  territories 2031 — the game prints no lyric text; the base's sung
  ghost-lead is flagged **AMBER for EU** in §13.
- **Why it belongs:** the Harukiya's own inheritance — the first
  Japanese pop generation's tenderest song: life is brief, fall in
  love, maidens. At native speed it closes throats; returned, a
  memento mori becomes a dance record about the same fact, which is
  somehow more honest. Never sung before midnight (§9); the etiquette
  is enforced by looks.

**A. BASE — 2/4, A minor, 76 BPM, 2:24.** In-fiction: cut 1925 in the
bar itself, after hours — the proprietor's mother, recorded once,
reluctantly, standing by the door as if to leave; violin; shamisen
doubling the line an octave under; the bar's upright, soft pedal down.
Three verses, the third hummed. Skeleton: her voice at −16 dB; violin
carries; the hummed verse stays fully voiced because the room hums it
with her and removing it broke every listener the fiction has. Slots:
three long falls (12-12-14). Kept incident: her breath before verse
one; the door latch at the end.

**B. THE RETURN — ×1.414, +6 → E♭ minor, 107.** The reluctant
contralto comes back a bright girl; the shamisen turns to glass; the
latch becomes a switch-click that ends the version like a light going
out. Returns of this song are received standing. Nobody has ever
requested one twice in the same night; once is understood to be the
correct amount.

- **The television shows:** the three falling slots and no prompt —
  the guide is famously unhelpful; someone has penciled "just listen
  first" in the binder and the TV faithfully reproduces the
  annotation.
- **The trace records:** singer, trio faint, and almost always a
  second low voice, because somebody always joins the hum. Traces of
  this song are never quite solo.
- **Generation brief:** record an intimate trio-and-contralto
  performance, 2/4, 76 BPM, A minor, instrumentation as specced, one
  close dry room mic, singer restrained and plain on the public-
  domain Japanese text (vocalise where territory clearance is in
  doubt), no edits, incidental sounds kept. **Negative constraints:**
  no vibrato coaching, no pads, no reverb tail past the room, no
  tuning, no "cinematic" anything, no fade.
- **Fictional provenance:** recorded by Kenji Uyeda, 31, piano tuner,
  Flushing, who maintains the bar's upright and set up the recorder
  as a birthday gift for the proprietor; the proprietor put the spool
  in the catalogue without asking his mother, which the family has
  argued about, gently, for three years. Spool: unlabeled — the only
  unlabeled spool in the binder, and everyone knows it.
- **Residents who choose it:** Mae Kessler (certifies nothing about
  it, sings it straight, will not discuss that); Malcolm Reed (hums
  the third verse to the F03 corridor fern, wrong key, every Sunday).
  Juno Kells has never chosen it and always stays for it.
- **Mundane anecdote:** the proprietor's mother has never listened to
  a return of her own base. She knows they exist. She asks, roughly
  monthly, whether people like them, and accepts the answer with a
  nod that closes the subject.
- **Rhea line (1 of at most 4):** her binder note for this page reads
  "no notes." It is the only page where she has written that.
- **Researched lineage:** Nakayama Shinpei and the birth of ryūkōka
  (Katyusha 1914, Gondola 1915 — research doc §I); the ballad-sped-
  whole practice of the upload era and its sincere reception (owner's
  reference compilation, §II). The fictional Uyeda and the
  proprietor's family are composites of no real persons.

### TRACK_103 — ALOHA OE AT CLOSING TIME

- **Source:** "Aloha ʻOe" — words and music Queen Liliʻuokalani, 1878,
  composed at Maunawili; autograph manuscript and her English
  translation preserved at the Hawaiʻi State Archives; UH Mānoa
  digital sheet collection. **PD: GREEN** (1878; d. 1917).
- **Why it belongs:** the most famous farewell in the world, written

### TRACK_104 — CHARLESTON!!!!!

- **Source:** "The Charleston" — music James P. Johnson, lyrics Cecil
  Mack, from *Runnin' Wild* (New Colonial Theatre, October 1923).
  Score: 1923 Harms sheet; LoC holdings. **PD: GREEN** (US 1923;

  piano — the pure DANCING slot. The 2007 test barely has to wake up:
  the Charleston returned at ×1.414 with a kick under it is a

  Charleston opinion; half are wrong on purpose.

whistle at bar 60.

chipmunk into MC hype and the matinée becomes a rave with no further
assistance; the stairwell kick grits up into a hardcore four; the
whistle turns piercing and the room whistles back at it. Returns of
Charleston takes are the loudest objects in the Songbook and the
binder's fiction says the neighbors have complained about a record
of people being happy, twice.

  Charleston footwork frame by frame, the most useful thing the

- **The trace records:** the caller, the HEYs (a Charleston trace is

  library crowd, no swing quantization, no tuning, no polish pass.
- **Fictional provenance:** recorded by the floor manager himself,
  Freddy DeLuca, 44, who wanted a practice record "faster than the

- **Rhea line (2 of at most 4):** the one song she'll do drunk — no

back a reedy, wounded tenor — the self-pity gets *younger*, which

a woodblock sprint; and the doubled rests, at speed, land at their

saving it.

- **The television shows:** refrain slots (5-7-5, flat-flat-fall)

- **Fictional provenance:** recorded by Ezra Okun, 38, the

  traces of this track outnumber all other days combined.
- **Generation brief:** record a six-bar acoustic drum break
  (brushes into sticks) alone in a hard stairwell; separately
  record voice/cornet/piano/guitar on the 1918 public-domain
  text, 120 BPM, Dm→F; assemble with hand-edit-style chops
  (re-order, displace, eight bars halftime under the title line)
  — splice logic, not DAW quantize. **Negative constraints:** no
  sampled or recreated famous breaks; no reese or sub synthesis;
  no sirens; no time-stretch; no vocal tuning.
- **Fictional provenance:** recorded by Marcus Vaughters, 30, the
  porter, who learned wire-editing from a signals manual left on
  a sleeper car and chopped his cousin's break "so the drums
  could miss somebody too." Label: VAUGHTERS & VAUGHTERS,
  STAIRWELL RECORDINGS — Elias's name first. Reached the bar via
  the coal man, Marcus's route friend.
- **Residents who choose it:** Cal Dwyer (the halftime is the
  only dance his balance trusts, and he owns the room in it);
  Lena (mends to the base on quiet nights; says the break "sews").
- **Rhea line (4 of at most 4):** she sings this one, once a
  season, entirely straight, and the binder's page carries her
  only performance note to herself: "let the drums do the
  crying."
- **Researched lineage:** Turner Layton and Henry Creamer's torch
  architecture (research doc §I); jungle's founding Black British
  break science — the chop-and-halftime school and the ragga-
  jungle blueprint (§V); and the Amen ethics that make this
  collection perform its own breaks — The Winstons' Gregory
  Coleman and Richard L. Spencer, uncompensated for the most
  sampled seconds in music (§V); the fictional label naming its
  drummer first is this proposal's answer. The Vaughters cousins
  are fictional and resemble no documented musicians.

### TRACK_111 — EAST SIDE WEST SIDE (ROUND AND ROUND)

- **Source:** "The Sidewalks of New York" — Charles B. Lawlor and
  James W. Blake, pub. 1894 (Howley, Haviland & Co.; the pair
  sold the copyright for $5,000); "East Side, West Side" by its
  chorus. The city's own waltz. **PD: GREEN** (US 1894; Lawlor
  d. 1925, Blake d. 1935 — clear everywhere).
- **Why it belongs:** the block's song — every borough claims it;
  the dancing-and-friendship slot for people who cannot dance,
  because it is a waltz and the whole room sways in threes. The
  return is the collection's precision experiment: a 3/4 record
  at nightcore speed spins rather than stomps, and the break
  underneath argues in twelves. "Round and round" becomes
  literal.

**A. BASE — 3/4, G major, 90 BPM, 2:52.** In-fiction: cut 1926 on
the Queensboro span's pedestrian walk at 2 a.m. (permit: none) by
a messenger-kid social club with a borrowed recorder — concertina,
two fiddles, banjo, a skate-wheel case drummed with palms, five
voices trading lines. Affinity layer: a second pass of the skate-
case drumming, wound in against the first at a displaced eighth,
so the waltz carries quiet 12/8 cross-figures — kid-mischief
polyrhythm, fully period. Skeleton: the five voices duck;
concertina guide; verse slots trade line-by-line (five singers or
one doing five voices; both traditions active). Kept incident:
bridge wind, two vehicle passes, one cop who audibly declines to
care and is thanked by name.

**B. THE RETURN — ×1.414, +6 → D♭, 127 in three.** Five kids come
back as one bright gang; the crossed skate-cases become a snare
argument from nowhere; the wind turns to whistle. Returned, the
track genuinely *spins* — regulars clear the middle of the floor
for it, and the turning dance that goes with it was invented by
Cam, credited to nobody, and is now simply how it is danced.

- **The television shows:** trade-off slots labeled by club
  nicknames, then a chorus card reading ALL OF YOU; prompt "NAME
  A STREET YOU MISS." Phrase-duel street-name cards land here by
  design.
- **The trace records:** up to five voices in relay, sway noise
  on the threes, the deep click of the skate case like a
  heartbeat with somewhere to be.
- **Generation brief:** record a five-voice street-band waltz,
  90 BPM, G major, on the 1894 public-domain text, outdoors with
  honest wind and two vehicle passes; overdub a second displaced
  skate-case pass for 12/8 crosses. **Negative constraints:** no
  4/4 conversion, no click, no wind removal, no library traffic,
  no sub-bass, no tuning.
- **Fictional provenance:** recorded by the Borough Hall Rollers
  — eight messenger kids, 14 to 19; treasurer Pearl Wachtel, 16,
  the only one who read the recorder's manual, billed the club
  two cents a splice. Spool: ROLLERS No. 1, label a delivery
  tag. The Rollers deliver the bar's ice, which is how
  everything they make arrives.
- **Residents who choose it:** Cam Ortiz (a courier singing the
  messenger kids' record is apostolic succession); the Ortiz
  table runs the five-voice relay at family occasions; Nadia
  Quell sings the "tripped the light fantastic" line with a
  planner's specific grief for pavements.
- **Mundane anecdote:** once a winter the bar pays the ice bill
  in performance instead of coin — eight kids, one horn, the
  binder's loudest night, the trace pinned by the door until the
  next one.
- **Researched lineage:** Lawlor and Blake's city waltz (research
  doc §I); jungle's two-tempos-in-love science, reapplied to
  three (§V); the documented family-and-pirate-radio thread of
  the jungle revival — preservation as household labor (§V).
  Pearl Wachtel and the Rollers are fictional; no real messenger,
  club or drummer is portrayed.

### TRACK_112 — THE ALPHABET SONG (BURNING BRIGHT)

- **Source:** "Oyfn Pripetshik" ("Der Alef-Beyz") — words and
  music Mark M. Warshawsky, Kyiv, 1890s; published 1901 with
  Sholem Aleichem's help; the cheder song — a teacher, a hearth,
  children learning the alphabet — so beloved it was mistaken for
  anonymous folk. Score: 1901 collection; Workers Circle Mlotek
  collection documentation. **PD: GREEN** (Warshawsky d. 1907 —
  clear everywhere).
- **Why it belongs:** family inheritance and labor in one: a song
  about the work of teaching, inherited by people who were taught
  with it — and deliberately NOT the collection's saddest track.
  This is a grandfather's song used to get a four-year-old to sit
  still, which is comedy. The gentlest return in the catalogue,
  chosen so the grandfather comes back *younger*, not silly.

**A. BASE — 2/4, D minor, 72 BPM, 3:05.** In-fiction: cut 1926 in
the back of a Grand Street seltzer depot by the route family that
owns it — grandfather's unhurried baritone ghost-lead; his
daughter's alto shadowing a sixth above on verse two; harmonium;
mandolin; and the depot's bottle racks, which ring sympathetically
on G and were left in. Affinity layer: a soft brushed pass on a
shipping crate, played by the daughter (she learned for this),
halftime throughout, one displacement per sixteen bars. Skeleton:
grandfather ducks; the alto shadow stays at half voice (singing
this alone is not the custom); mandolin guide. The binder marks
it DUET, GENERATIONS PREFERRED. Kept incident: cart wheels
outside; the harmonium's leak; the child the record was made for,
audibly failing to sit still at bar 40, kept by unanimous family
vote.

**B. THE RETURN — ×1.26, +4 → F♯ minor, 91.** The catalogue's
gentlest ratio: the grandfather returns as a young man, the
bottle racks become a glockenspiel nobody owns, the crate-brush
halftime turns liquid, and the child's wiggle becomes a giggle
that half the bar now waits for. Returns of this track are how
the fiction's parents get *their* children to sit still, which
the grandfather calls "the bribe, industrialized."

- **The television shows:** verse slots (10-10-8, all falling)
  and — uniquely — an alphabet drawn one letter per bar along the
  bottom, any alphabet, the terminal cycling scripts between
  performances; prompt "TEACH SOMEBODY SOMETHING."
- **The trace records:** two voices a generation apart, the brush
  faint, the bottle-glock shimmer — Alphabet traces are instantly
  recognizable in the drawer.
- **Generation brief:** record baritone-and-alto with harmonium,
  mandolin, sympathetic glass resonance and a brushed crate
  halftime pass, 2/4 at 72, D minor, on the public-domain Yiddish
  text. **Negative constraints:** no klezmer ornament pack, no
  minor-key melodrama, no strings, no choir, no tuning, no
  removing the child.
- **Fictional provenance:** recorded by Chana Perl, 34 — Perl &
  Daughter, Seltzer — so her son would sit for the alphabet the
  way she never did; her father requests the spool by its family
  name, "the bribe." Label: PERL & DAUGHTER — cheder tempo.
  Reached the Harukiya on the seltzer route, where everything
  eventually does.
- **Residents who choose it:** Lena Ortiz and Evelyn Marsh as the
  binder's standing grandmother-register duet (one mends, one
  taught; the song is both); Jonah Price hums it at the desk on
  nights the notebook stays shut — a beginning song that asks
  for no ending.
- **Mundane anecdote:** the child on the base, now seven in the
  fiction, has performed it at the bar once — over his own
  little cousin wiggling. The binder's genealogy page for this
  track and the family's actual tree are the same diagram, and
  nobody has noticed.
- **Researched lineage:** Warshawsky and the Yiddish song world
  that loved his work into anonymity (research doc §I); liquid
  jungle's soft-break grammar (§V); the household-preservation
  thread of the jungle revival (§V). The Perl family are
  fictional seltzer people and resemble no documented musicians.

## 12. SIX RESERVES — short pitches

1. **DAISY BELL (GO FASTER)** — "Daisy Bell (A Bicycle Built for
   Two)," Harry Dacre, 1892. **GREEN** (Dacre d. 1922). Base: a
   cycling club's parlor waltz with a bell choir of actual bicycle
   bells. Return ×1.414: the proposal's second 3/4 spin, giddier
   and dumber than track_111. Reserve because two waltz returns is
   one too many for Volume One; first substitution when a waltz
   slot opens. Register: friendship, courtship by committee.
2. **LA PALOMA (COME BACK TO ME)** — Sebastián Iradier, ~1863.
   **GREEN** (d. 1865). Base: habanera duo — guitar, voice, one
   conga-substitute crate — cut in a Chelsea boarding-house
   kitchen. Return ×1.335: the habanera lilt at speed is halfway
   to a broken-beat and needs no help. Register: migration,
   promised returns. Reserve pending a Spanish-language phrase-
   guide pass done properly.
3. **MOLIHUA (JASMINE ALL NIGHT)** — "Molihua," traditional
   Chinese melody, documented in print since the 18th century
   (Barrow, 1804). **GREEN** (traditional; arrangement ours).
   Base: erhu, yangqin and a grandmother-granddaughter unison cut
   above a Mott Street tea shop. Return ×1.26, gentle, per the
   track_112 rule: elders return younger, never silly. Register:
   inheritance. Reserve pending a proper consultation pass on
   romanization and phrase-guide language.
4. **SANTA LUCIA (ROW US HOME)** — Teodoro Cottrau's Neapolitan
   barcarolle, 1849. **GREEN** (d. 1879). Base: a longshoreman

   Return ×1.335: barcarolle sway becomes a hands-locked side-to-
   side the whole room can do drunk. Register: labor's morning

   track_103.
5. **THE PRISONER'S SONG (IF I HAD WINGS)** — credited Guy Massey,

   (8, 10); rotate in when one rotates out.
6. **LA GOLONDRINA (THE SWALLOW)** — Narciso Serradell Sevilla,

   Avenue shop. Return ×1.335: the era's great migration farewell
   returns as flight itself. Register: exile, tenderness.

## 13. LEGAL POLICY AND THE BLOCKED LIST

**Policy.** Primary jurisdiction is US publication ≤1930 (all
twelve cores are US-PD). Author-death life+70 status is checked
per track: **GREEN** = clear in US and life+70 territories;
**AMBER** = US-clear, encumbered somewhere major (named per
dossier: 2 — EU lyrics only, to 2031; 7 — EU to 2046; 9 — EU to
2031; 10 — EU to 2049). AMBER ships nowhere without an owner
territory decision; every AMBER dossier survives demotion to
reserve without collapsing the collection. Genuinely uncertain
status is BLOCKED, not rationalized. Composition and recording
rights are never conflated: no historical recording is used
anywhere, so only composition status is in play.

**Blocked:**

- **'O sole mio** (di Capua, 1898): the 2002 Turin ruling
  recognized Alfredo Mazzucchi as co-composer, extending Italian
  protection to 2042. US-PD but multi-jurisdiction-encumbered by
  court ruling — the exact case the block rule exists for.
- **Cielito Lindo** (Quirino Mendoza y Cortés, 1882): composer
  died 1957; Mexico's term is life+100 — protected in its home
  jurisdiction until the 2050s. Blocked despite US-PD status; a
  Mexican farewell slot is served by La Golondrina (reserve 6),
  which is clear everywhere.
- **Tiger Rag** (pub. 1917, ODJB credit): authorship contested
  between the copyright registrants and documented New Orleans
  oral history. The paper is US-PD; the provenance is not a story
  this collection can tell honestly in one label line. Blocked on
  attribution integrity, not term.
- **My Yiddishe Momme** (Yellen/Pollack, 1925): Jack Yellen died
  1991 — encumbered outside the US for decades yet. Blocked; the
  Yiddish inheritance slot is served by track_112, clear
  everywhere.

## 14. THE LISTENING LINE — nondiegetic credits proposal

An extras-menu feature (settings/credits tier, alongside the
music credits the game already owes), explicitly outside the
fiction. One screen per Volume One track plus one scene overview
page. Per-track card, top to bottom:

1. **The song:** real title, writer(s), publication year, one
   sentence of documented history, and the archival score source
   (Levy / LoC / NYPL / Hawaiʻi State Archives / Workers Circle,
   linked).
2. **The fiction:** the in-game base and its made-up provenance,
   clearly labeled *"The performers and recordists in the game
   are fictional."*
3. **The listening line:** 1-3 real artists or scenes whose
   documented practice informed the treatment family, each with
   one concrete sentence about what was learned and an official
   listen/purchase link (artist site, label page, or artist-run
   Bandcamp — storefronts only where official pages are gone).
4. **The separation line, verbatim on every card:** *"These
   artists and communities inspired our research. They did not
   participate in this game and do not endorse it. Buy their
   records."*

Scene overview page: short, plain paragraphs on the nightcore
upload era, UK breakbeat hardcore/happy hardcore, jungle's Black
British foundations and revival, hyperpop/digicore, and the
cross-scene practices (T4T rave lineage, Deaf Rave's felt-sound
practice, accessibility organizing) — each with the same link
discipline. UI: binder-adjacent paper stock, no diegetic
typefaces, ratio of each seeded version printed as "returned at
×1.335" so even the credits keep the two layers separate.

## 15. SELF-AUDIT (per the commission's checklist)

- **Phonautograph contradictions:** none found against §III.2 as
  amended — the horn never plays; returns are terminal/reader
  behavior; the ratio is immutable; found traces alone keep
  instability. §2 carries the boundary table.
- **Accidental recorded-vocal playback in the bar:** base ghost-
  leads and returned versions are catalog audio by canon;
  uncommanded voices remain the Tenant; no dossier plays a
  singer's clean stem, ever.
- **WORS/Songbook conflation:** none — WORS is never a source or
  destination for any spool; the WORS catalog's *register* is
  cited for anecdote voice only.
- **Unsupported identity claims:** every identity statement in
  the lineage entries traces to self-description or strong
  public documentation, cited in the research doc; where thin,
  omitted (research doc §VII lists the omissions).
- **Copyright uncertainty:** GREEN/AMBER/BLOCKED policy in §13;
  four AMBERs flagged, four blocks named, no rationalized cases.
- **Direct artist imitation:** generation briefs contain zero
  artist names and zero style-of phrasing; checked line by line.
- **Tokenism / misery balance:** registers audited in §10 —
  euphoria, comedy and labor-joy outnumber grief; no lineage
  entry exists for demographics alone; no fictional participant
  is a tragedy delivery device.
- **Repetitive structures:** twelve bases span waltz, 2/4 ballad,
  hapa haole, cut-time stomp, duet, crowd unison, torch quintet,
  deadpan, novelty, break-led torch, 3/4 street band, family
  lullaby; returns span ×1.26-×1.414; two tracks share a form
  only where the social use differs (5/6 crowd energy vs duet).
- **Fictional artists vs real people:** all twenty-odd named
  fictional participants checked against the lineage list for
  accidental portraiture; no name, biography or signature
  matches a documented person.
- **Brief vagueness:** every generation brief specifies meter,
  key, tempo, ensemble, room, text source, incident policy and
  negative constraints; no "glitchy," "ethereal," "diverse" or
  "underground" anywhere in a spec.
